# Shiftland: game design

**Shiftland** (concept name "Plates"), picked by Victor on 2026-11-03. This is the design the build follows; changes are recorded here.

## One line

A small island in the ocean is cut into tectonic plates. Slide a plate along its rail: where it pushes into a neighbour the land rises into mountains, where it pulls away a rift opens and the sea pours in. Make every creature on the island happy in as few shifts as you can.

## The first five minutes (what a rater sees)

1. Title: the island bobbing in the ocean, plates gently breathing; "Play" is the only button that matters.
2. Level 1 (one plate, one goat, one move): the goat looks at the neighbouring plate; the plate's rail arrow pulses. One drag: columns rise with a rumble, the goat hops onto the peak and bleats. Done in 10 seconds; the verb is learned without text.
3. Level 2: pulling a plate away opens a rift; the sea pours in and a buoy's fish jump. Water is learned.
4. Level 3: a goat wants a peak, and the obvious push raises it, but the same push lands on the neighbour's village and lifts it to height 2: houses tilt and crack, undo glows. The first "think first" moment. (Villages ride their plate, so the rules can crush a village but never drown one; Codex review, chain 2.)
5. Level 4: push in, pull back, and the plate keeps the peak it stole and leaves sea under the buoy. Level 5, Good Neighbours: the goat's own plate cannot lift it, so the answer starts with a move that ticks nothing and lets the neighbour take the cell in front of the goat. The first real "aha": `greedySolveRate` is 0, so following the preview alone cannot find it (design audit Nov 4, D2).

By minute 5 the rater has used every core rule, had one failure that was funny rather than punishing, and solved one real puzzle.

The Meadow as shipped (`game/src/levels/tutorial.ts`; the whole set is in "Level set" below):

The solver found the rule behind levels 4, 5 and 9 when it beat a hand-made level by pushing a plate twice and pulling it back twice: **a plate carries its raised land and creatures with it** (a mountain made by a push stays a mountain when the plate slides back).

## Level progression (the plan; the shipped list is "Level set" below)

The original plan, kept for its reasoning: levels 1-11 plus level 14 as the one Finale level, with Tundra (12-13) and the second Finale level (15) as the stretch (see "Scope decision"). On Nov 4 the set grew to 20 levels with Tundra in, then to 24 after the first-player playtest, then to 25 with Sandbar after playtest 3, then to 28 after playtest 4 (Sandbar out, four new islands): the numbers and some names in this table are the plan's, not the shipped order.

Unlocking (decided Nov 3, `game/src/game/progress.ts`): the tutorials (`role: tutorial`, the leading run) open strictly in order, because each teaches what the next relies on. From the first puzzle on, two unsolved levels are always open, so a player stuck on one puzzle can play the next and come back (Play reopens the first unsolved one). Why: jam raters who hit a wall stop playing and never see the later biomes they would rate; a strict chain makes the hardest puzzle a quit point. Cost: a player can reach the Finale with one level skipped. Nov 4: that one level can be a biome's teacher (Thin Ice, say), and the puzzles after it rely on what it shows, because only the leading run opens in order and that run now ends at level 4. Closing this means `unlocked` also waits on every earlier `role: tutorial` level.

Every beat in this table is written as a solver fixture before its level is built: the level's intended solution, the trap move and the "aha" move are asserted by `npm run levels:audit` (S5).

Rules for the list: every `role: puzzle` level passes the full quality bar (`audit`: par ≥ 3, order matters, a tempting wrong move, land and plates kept, no goal that starts met); a teacher (`role: tutorial`) is exempt from "par ≥ 3" but keeps the trap its fixture names, and a late teacher is followed at once by a puzzle that uses its rule (the one exception is Sandbar, see "Why the Desert and Tundra changed"); a new biome's twist ships only once the rules module, solver and a unit test support it; if a twist misses its date, its levels fall back to Meadow/Coast rules and the twist is cut (12 good levels beat 15 average ones).

**The ramp after the Nov 4 playtest.** The first player wrote "Nothing had stopped me in the first six", then met Two Waters at 13 as a wall. The shape now: teachers stay at 1-2 moves (Anchor Rock is the one at 3, on purpose: it tests the Sandbar bite under the pin), and every biome before the Finale climbs from its teachers to a closer of par 4 or 5. Good Neighbours (5) is the first aha, High Pasture (6) the first level a thinking player has to stop at, and Shepherd's Hill (7) combines the Meadow's lessons. Oxbow now stands between the Desert's teachers and Two Waters. Effort (see "Level set") runs 40, 98, 172 through the Meadow's puzzles, 33, 103, 81 on the Coast, 19 (Sandbar), 105, 125, 198 in the Desert, 42 (Anchor Rock), 48, 87, 196 in the Tundra, and 554, 1292, 1689 in the Finale.

**Why these levels (Nov 4 rework after the first-player playtest).**

- Dropped: Far One First (its title solved it, and two pushes were no aha) and Ice Dam (effort 22: it fell to following ticks; its fact, a pond is not the sea, moved to Frost Line's blurb).
- Renamed because the title was an instruction or said nothing: Mind the Village is Village Green, Dip and Return is Harbour Row, Wind-Up is Salt Point, Step Aside is Cold Feet.
- High Pasture: the opening's stop. The obvious push gives the first goat its peak and swallows the second; a quiet move steps the second goat out of the row, and it comes back to climb the edge the push raised. The trap is dead, and proven so.
- Shepherd's Hill: three taught lessons at once, and a tick worth nothing (P2N floats the buoy and costs 2 moves over par).
- Good Neighbours and The Long Pier: new maps with no pond, since ponds used to appear before lakes are taught. Low Tide: new map whose trap ticks two goals and still leaves the goat nothing to climb.
- Oxbow: one rift split by a stub of land into sea and lake, the bridge the tester asked for before Two Waters. The second player was stuck on it for 12 minutes: its pond touched the sea at a corner, a fact Two Waters only states one level later. Swapping the two would undo the first player's fix, because Two Waters in that slot was the wall and its answer needs the corner fact too. So two cells changed instead: a gains a cell at the rift's north end, which slides to the pond's corner so the pond touches no sea even there, and c takes a's south-west cell, which cuts tick-following from 1668 tries to 426. The wrong start P2N still finishes in 7 moves at best (was 8). Whether this is enough needs another player: one tester's stump, and the new reasons on the chips arrive at the same time.
- Thin Ice: was par 1 this late, the tester's worst level. Now the first push does not melt and the second does, so it shows the threshold (height 2) instead of stating it.
- Thaw: ties the Coast's rule to the Tundra's. Ice crossing water stays frozen while the cell beside it climbs, melts and opens the sea.
- Frost Line: the Tundra's closer. Every Tundra idea on one rock, with a retreat that ticks two goals and costs 2 moves over par.
- High Water: bridges Frost Line and Give and Take. A par-5 Finale opener with no ice, so the Coast and Desert rules return after the Tundra. Found by search (1.1M maps tried, 5 kept), then traced by hand. Without it Give and Take was 6.6x the hardest level before it.
- The Summit (redrawn Nov 4; the old map was par 6, effort 44, the weakest late level): every creature and the rock, and three stops on the way. The easy slide P0E ticks four on the first move, and it is the level's last move too early, since a backs away from the only land that can lift the goat. Fetching the goat and coming straight home (P0W P0W P0E P0E P0E) is five of six, with a's tail on the buoy where b cannot reach it. So b has to bite the tail off while a is out at the rock, and a cannot come home until b has gone back north with it, because the village would land on the penguin (refused). The goal count on the line runs 1 3 4 5 5 4 3 6: two goals given back on the way home, then one slide ticks oasis, lighthouse and penguin together. It keeps the old map's best moment, five of six and then giving back. Found by a search built around that hand-drawn shape, then trimmed by hand and mirrored. It is par 7 and not 8 because `orderMatters` in `solver/audit.ts` only permutes up to 7 moves, so a par-8 level fails the audit whatever it does.

**Why the Desert and Tundra changed (Nov 4, after playtest 3; Sandbar has since gone and Mirage has moved, see the next block).** The tester found that biting into a neighbour and retreating with the land is what Two Waters, Mirage and High Water want, that nothing teaches it, and that the hardest islands played (15-16 then) were followed by the two easiest (17-18). Their report carries its own warning: they jumped from 5 to 15, so they missed the Coast's three in-and-back puzzles, and they never really tried 8 or 12. So the spike may be smaller for a player who came the long way, and the "par 1 after par 3-4" note on 8 and 12 rests on the numbers alone. Those two are unchanged: each opens a biome with a new creature in about ten seconds.

- Sandbar (new, slot 14): the buoy sits on b's land, and b cannot slide clear of it. Stolen Peak (4) already has the same two moves, but there the bite ticks the goat and the retreat ticks the buoy, so a player follows ticks and never sees why the water came. Here the bite ticks nothing and looks worse, because it heaps land on the buoy. The only tick on offer is P0W, and it is a trap. All the water comes from the retreat. An exhaustive search finds no solution at any length without a retreat (`solveWithoutRetreat`), and `greedySolveRate` is 0.
- Anchor Rock (redrawn, par 2 to 3): "the same slide twice" is gone. The buoy is on the rock, so Sandbar's bite is the first thing a player tries, and it breaks the village beside it, because the rock cannot step aside. The answer changes the biter instead: c takes the front of a, so a's bite misses the village. It also shows the other half of the idea, that a bite changes the shape of the plate it took from, which is what The Summit and High Water rely on.
- Thin Ice moved ahead of Anchor Rock, so the Tundra climbs par 2, 3, 3, 3, 5 (effort 10, 42, 48, 87, 196) where it was 2, 2, 3, 3, 5 (11, 10, 48, 87, 196). One short teacher now follows Mirage, not two. The drop from 198 to 10 remains, because a new rule still needs a plain first showing.
- Not done: moving Two Waters and Mirage after Frost Line, as the tester suggested. Grouped by biome, that means either relabelling them Finale, where the par would fall from 5 to 3 and the Finale's 5-7 rule would break, or swapping the Desert and the Tundra, which would show ponds in the Tundra before lakes are taught, and Frost Line leans on knowing a pond from the sea. Oxbow also stays before Two Waters, for the reason in the first playtest's notes above.
- The exception to "followed at once by a puzzle that uses its rule": Oxbow follows Sandbar and does not use it. Putting Sandbar straight before Two Waters makes par fall inside the biome (3, 2, 3) and splits nothing useful, so it stays at slot 14, with Breakwater's cap one level from Oxbow. Mirage (17) is the first puzzle that needs the literal bite and retreat; its fixture now proves `carries`. Two Waters (16) uses the other half: b bites c's tail, so c's slide leaves the buoy clear. Nothing teaches that half before Two Waters except the idea that a bite takes land off its plate. Watch this first in the next playtest.

Soft spots we chose to keep after playtest 3, with that day's numbers, to watch in play. Sandbar falls to random play about as often as Stolen Peak (31.1% of 6-move walks, against 30.5%), though every such walk does bite and retreat, and it counts as a teacher's number. The Finale steps are the biggest in the set: 196 to 554 is the steepest ratio (2.8x), 554 to 1292 the biggest jump (2.3x), and 1292 to 1689 (1.3x) lets the last level stand on its idea, not on being far harder. The Summit's first three moves all tick, so a player following ticks can reach five of six without seeing why b bites the tail; what stops them is the walk home, which gives goals back. Plate c never moves on its line: it is only the pond's wall, so the climax is really a and b. Coast puzzles lean on push-in and pull-back. Harbour Row (33) is the easiest puzzle left, a deliberately low restart after Shepherd's Hill (172).

**Why the middle changed (Nov 4, after playtest 4).** The tester: "A good puzzle game is hiding inside a pleasant one, but its middle sags. Islands 8-14 almost never made me think twice." Thaw was their best island: the obvious plan works, and the chip then says the water is the wrong kind. So each island from 8 to 19 was asked what a player tries first, what the board answers, and what they know afterwards that they did not know before. Moves named in the older notes above are from before these mirrors.

- Thaw's shape, a plan that succeeds and a chip that says why it was not enough, now also carries Backwater (the bite and retreat floats the buoy, the oasis chip says "that water reaches the sea"), Twin Palms and Wishing Well. Two Waters already had it in its trap.
- New: The Cut (10), Twin Palms (16), Backwater (18), Wishing Well (19). Gone: Sandbar, which the tester called a recap: Stolen Peak has the same two moves, and The Cut and Twin Palms now show carried land inside a real puzzle.
- Retuned for fewer accidents: Salt Point, Low Tide, Breakwater, Thin Ice, Anchor Rock. Still Water, Cold Feet and Thaw are unchanged. Long Pier and Still Water stay as ten-second biome openers.
- Mirage went from Desert slot 17 to first in the Finale. It was a spike with three easier islands after it. Now the Desert closes on Wishing Well, par 5, and the Finale runs par 4, 5, 6, 7. Moving it past the Tundra also lets the Finale open by bringing lakes back after five islands of ice.
- Numbers. Random solve from Harbour Row to Anchor Rock, leaving out Still Water, which opens a biome in one move: the mean fell from 12.7% to 6.7%, and the worst from 31.1% (Sandbar) to 14.5% (Low Tide). Coast effort is now 4, 32, 97, 116, 137 (was 4, 33, 103, 81, a fall at its last island). Desert: 3, 22, 139, 103, 121, 116, 122 (was 3, 16, 19, 105, 125, 198). Tundra: 10, 48, 48, 87, 190. Finale: 218, 569, 1286, 1738.
- Oxbow stays ahead of Twin Palms, though the effort number says otherwise, because it is the first to ask for both waters and Twin Palms assumes ponds. The effort heuristic also moves by up to about 20% when a map is only mirrored, so it cannot separate these.

Soft spots from this round, to watch in play. 28 islands is long for a jam game: Twin Palms or Harbour Row should go first if it needs to shrink. The Desert's puzzles are five near-equal efforts (103-139), with different ideas but the same size. Wishing Well's effort is low for par 5, because it has few boards, so a patient player can walk it. Harbour Row is still the easiest puzzle and its aha is a quiet move. Thin Ice's wall no longer stands straight at the start, which is a less plain first showing of melting. Low Tide's 14.5% sits just under the bar. Two Waters hides the corner it is about behind the tip of c (see its comment in `campaign.ts`), and the two waters read apart by colour instead.

**How a level is proven.** Each spec carries a fixture, and `npm run levels:audit` (`game/src/solver/fixtures.ts`) checks it against the rules: the intended solution solves at par, is a shortest solution and equals the exported `sol`; the aha move is on every shortest path and, on some turn of the line, does what the fixture claims (`lifts`, `melts`, `seals`, `returns`, ...); the trap is a legal first move off every shortest path that costs exactly what the fixture says, where "dead" must be proven, never "nothing within the search depth". The dead proof is the in-game warning's own `canFinish` at its own cap (300k states), so a player in a dead trap is always told; High Pasture's trap is the slowest, 272k states and about 5 s in the worker. Every goal, stretch of ice and pin must change the answer (the decoy test), and a puzzle must not fall to greedy play. Meadow and Coast levels may not show a pond on the intended line or on any one-move preview from it, because lakes are taught in the Desert (`lakesBeforeTheyAreTaught`). Shape checks reject a level over 9x9, land on the ocean ring, a plain rectangle, a plate with no room, a plate in two pieces (it slides as one, so it would read as two plates), a goat on ice. Whether a trap looks like progress and whether a sentence reads true are judgement, not code: those go through the design audit.

**Effort** (`effortOf` in `game/src/solver/audit.ts`): the ramp's number, how many moves a player spends who does not read the board. It is the cheaper of two strategies, averaged over 24 seeds: following ticks (best-first by goals met, skipping states with a crushed village, capped at 4000) and trying blind (breadth-first in random order). It exists because par and random solve missed what the playtest found: Ice Dam, par 3, fell in 22 tries. Ticks count even with the preview's badges gone after level 3, because a made move still ticks its chip and undo is free. `audit.test.ts` holds the ramp: no puzzle under 30, the Meadow's three puzzles climb, the first level at 100 or more comes before level 8, par never falls inside a biome, and no puzzle after the first is above 3x the hardest level before it.

**Random solve** (`node src/solver/cli.ts random`): the share of 2000 random walks of 3 x par moves that hit a solved state. It is the ramp's sanity check, not the ramp itself: teachers run up to 70%, puzzles stay under 15%, the Finale under 3%. Each biome restarts easy because its first levels teach a new rule.

**Greedy solve** (same command): the exact chance that a player who always takes the move ticking the most goals, with no undo, finishes in par moves, over every tie-break. It answers "does the preview just tell you the solution?". Teachers may be 100%; puzzles stay at 15% or under (only Cold Feet is above 0, at 12.5%). The first level at 0 is Good Neighbours, slot 5, because raters mostly see the first five levels.

**Read from the camera.** The camera sits at +x,+z, so a raised cell hides what lies north and west of it. Rotating or mirroring a map keeps its proof (the moves turn with it), so choose the orientation by measuring, then rendering the start and the solved state. `solver/occlusion.ts` walks view rays through the rules' own heights with the game's lens, on the landscape and phone screens and beside the solved panel, and `npm run levels:audit` fails a level when a creature or a plate is 50% hidden or more on its start, on any board of its intended line, on the trap's first board or on the solved board. A spec's `hiddenOk` can excuse that with a written reason; no level uses it. Reason from the solved heights of every plate, not one ridge: mirroring one map often moves its ridge behind another creature. The anchor rock's cell comes from `plateAnchor` in `render/island.ts` (nearest the plate's centre, away from creature lines), so it moves with the map: look at it too.

## Rules (as in `spikes/plates/plates.ts`)

- Square grid, an island inside an ocean ring. Every cell is either plate land (plate id, height 0-3) or water.
- A move slides one whole plate one cell along its rail (each plate has one axis, shown as a rail; travel limited to ±2 from its start, shown as rail end-stops).
- Convergent: where the moving plate lands on another plate's cell, it takes the cell and the height becomes max(both) + 1, capped at 3. Whatever stands there is lifted with it.
- Divergent: cells the plate leaves become water.
- Water joined to the ocean is **sea**; water enclosed by land is a **lake** (different colour; matters from the desert biome on). Water joins edge to edge only: two holes touching at a corner stay apart, so a lake can sit corner to corner with the sea.
- A plate may move into ocean-ring cells (reclaiming sea); it may not leave the map.
- No stacking (design audit S4): a push that would put two creatures on one cell is blocked; the plate shakes and the rail end flashes. Before this rule, 23 of 28 par solutions moved a creature across plates and 5 needed two creatures on one cell. Taking a cell with a creature on it is still allowed (the creature now rides the mover). Checked in `selfCheck()`.
- The game never removes a creature (S6): a move that would melt ice under a creature is blocked too, so every level's goal list is fixed from start to finish.
- Undo and restart are free and unlimited. Move count is shown against **par** (the solver's minimum); finishing at par earns a star. Stars are optional mastery, never a gate.

## Biomes and twists

| Biome | Levels | New idea | Why it adds depth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meadow | 1-7 | plates, uplift, rifts, goats, villages, buoys, carrying a peak | the base verb |
| Coast | 8-12 | lighthouses (need coast) and ocean-ring reclaiming | moving land into the sea, not just splitting it |
| Desert | 13-19 | lakes vs sea (oasis wants a lake, buoys want sea); a bite carries land off (Twin Palms) | the same rift must be closed off or opened to the ocean |
| Tundra | 20-24 | pinned plates (an anchor rock: cannot move, only be pushed into) and ice cells (melt to water when lifted to height ≥ 2) | reverses expectations of uplift |
| Finale | 25-28 | all of the above on a larger island, par 4-7 | the payoff |

Each twist ships only if the solver and audit support it before levels are made with it.

Twist spike (`node spikes/plates/twists.ts 150`, 2026-11-03): each twist added to 150 rails levels with seam placement, solved to depth 6. "Tight" = par ≥ 3 with ≤ 4 shortest solutions (a quick depth proxy, weaker than the full audit).

| Variant | Solvable ≤ 6 | Par 4-6 | Tight |
|---|---|---|---|
| No twist | 98 | 32 | 43 |
| Pinned plate | 64 | 16 | 26 |
| Tundra (pinned plate + 3 ice cells + penguin) | 42 | 17 | 24 |
| Desert (oasis wants a lake) | 43 | 23 | 35 |

(Rerun after the S4 no-stacking and S6 no-melting-creatures rules, with ice counted as terrain so creatures may stand on it (Codex chain 3); the first run, before them, had 110/74/61/62 solvable and 46/32/35/50 tight. Desert stays the strongest twist; Tundra is the thinnest and is first in the cut order.)

All three twists make puzzles; none is dead weight. The oasis pushes levels deeper (the lake/sea tension is the strongest twist), so Desert is the biome to keep if one is cut. Pinned plates remove options, so they suit early Tundra levels; ice adds unsolvable layouts, so ice levels need curation rather than raw generation. Rules as spiked: a pinned plate never moves but can be pushed into; ice melts to water when its cell is lifted to height ≥ 2, and a move that would melt ice under a creature is blocked (S6: the first spike deleted the creature and its goal, so a level could be won by melting it); penguins want an ice cell that touches sea; an oasis wants an adjacent lake. `selfCheck()` covers melt, pinned, push-into-pinned and the blocked melt under a penguin; `goals()` throws if a goal ever disappears.

## Controls

- Mouse: hover a plate to highlight it and show its rail; drag along the rail; while dragging, a **preview** shows the exact result (ghost columns rising, blue water fill including far-off flooding) before release. Release commits, Esc cancels.
- Keyboard: Tab / 1-9 cycles plates, arrow keys move along the rail, Z undo, R restart, Enter next level.
- Touch: tap a plate, then swipe along the rail; same preview while the finger is down.
- The rail is a row of move steps, not a slider (S1): it looks reversible but is not, because moving back takes cells again and raises them. Each step shows the ghost of the land it will take, and level 6 teaches "land remembers" before any level relies on it.
- Stuck states are never silent (S3): after every move `canFinish` searches every reachable state (no depth limit, capped at 300k states) for a solved one; only a proven dead end shows "no way back from here" and makes undo glow, and hitting the cap shows nothing. A village on a peak counts as proven at once, because a cell's height only ever rises. (A depth-limited search called far-but-solvable states dead ends: Codex chain 3, regression test in `game/src/rules/rules.test.ts`.) The check runs in a worker so input never waits. Prototyped in the grey-box.

## Feedback and feel

- Uplift: columns ease up over ~350 ms with a low rumble, small camera shake, dust puffs at the seam.
- Rift: a crack sound, the seam splits, the water front pours in from the nearest sea edge over ~500 ms (flood fill animated by distance).
- Goal ticks with a rising chime; the creature reacts; the last goal triggers the level-complete sequence (camera pull-back, all creatures celebrate, par star).
- Fail states are funny and soft: a crushed village's houses tilt with a comic creak and the villagers hop onto the roofs; undo is one key.

## Look and sound

- Look: `concepts/plates-key-v3.png` and the `shiftland-*.png` sheets: the island sits in an animated ocean (not over clouds), plate tints plus edge patterns (colour-blind safe), soft shadows, four biome palettes. All geometry and textures made in code.
- Plate palette rule: plate tints never use blue, teal or cyan (hue ~170-250°): water owns those hues (sea turquoise, lake green-teal), so a plate must never read as water. (Found in the first biome sheet, where a pale-blue dotted plate read as water.)
- Plate identity (taste critic T1/T3, 2026-11-03): each plate is a bold perimeter band plus a large surface pattern, the same in gameplay, title art and cover, and legible in greyscale at play size: plate 1 coral band + diagonal stripes, plate 2 golden band + round dots, plate 3 violet band + zigzag chevrons (plate 4 in the finale: rust band + checks). The biome changes only the ground cover inside the band (grass, sand, snow), and that ground cover is tinted toward the plate's hue (coral-, golden-, lilac-tinted grass) so plates separate at a glance; the pattern is low-contrast relief that still reads in greyscale but never out-shouts the rail, the preview marks or the creatures (HUD sheet critic T6). Creature states each carry one status badge (green check / red cross) on top of a silhouette or pose change, so they read at 40 px (T4).
- Uplift art follows the rules (S10): the mover's cell rises to max + 1 as one column; there is no buckling ridge across both plates, and the concept sheets' wider ridges are artistic licence the build does not copy.
- Props follow the biome: no cacti in Meadow, no pines in Desert.
- Sound: WebAudio synth only. A gentle marimba/pad loop per biome (pentatonic, so moves can add notes in key), SFX: rumble, crack, water pour, goat bleat (formant synth), chimes, UI clicks. Mute and volume in settings.

## Grey-box (2026-11-03)

`spikes/plates/play.html` (open from disk; rebuild with the command at the top of `play.ts`) is a top-down playable test of the rules with 14 levels (the 6 tutorial levels from `tutorial.ts`, then 8 audited generated puzzles at par 3-4, re-exported after the S2/S4/S8 changes). Click or Tab to select a plate, arrows to slide, Shift + arrow to preview, Z undo, R restart, H solver hint.

Checked headless (Playwright): every level solved at par through the real key handling, no console errors. Screens: `D:\Work\_local\evidence\2026-11-03-gameoff-plates\` (warm-up 1, puzzle 5 with preview, puzzle 5 solved).

What it taught:
- A full ghost overlay of the next state is hard to read (creatures appear twice, everything tints). The real preview should draw only the **difference**: rising columns, new water, creatures whose goal flips (with a tick or cross). (Done in the grey-box: Shift + arrow now marks rising cells with their new height, new water with a hatch, and goal flips with +/− badges; screens `greybox-diff-preview-l3.png` and `-l4.png` in the evidence folder.)
- Generated solutions often let one plate eat most of another (puzzle 5: the orange plate shrinks to a third). It reads as conquest, not shaping; the level filter should also cap how much of any one plate is consumed, or hand-made levels should avoid it. (Done: the audit requires every plate to keep ≥ 50% of its cells, `minPlateKept`.)
- Rails that run off the island into the ocean read well: the plate visibly has room to move.
- Plate ids in hints ("P1E") are meaningless to players; the real hint highlights the plate and arrow.
